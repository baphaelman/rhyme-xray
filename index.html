          How Rhyme X-ray works

          Eight steps turn the verse into colours. Open a step to read the rule on the left and see, on the right, what it did to the verse you are looking at.

          1Sounds, not spellingevery word becomes sounds, shown in IPA

          Spelling lies about rhyme: through, though and tough share letters and no sounds. So each word is looked up in the CMU Pronouncing Dictionary (135,000 words).

          Lyric spellings are normalised first. "spinnin'" is read as "spinning" with the g dropped, "tha" as "the", "'em" as /əm/, "2020" as "twenty twenty". Plurals, -ed, -ing, -er and -y forms are built from their base word.

          Anything still unknown is guessed from its spelling with a few dozen rules and gets a dotted underline, so you can see where the tool is unsure.

          2Choosing a readingwhen a word has more than one pronunciation

          8,444 dictionary words can be said more than one way: REcord and reCORD, a /laɪv/ show and to /lɪv/, "read" now and "read" yesterday. The dictionary lists them in no reliable order, so the first entry can't be trusted.

          Grammar first. Words whose stress moves take the verb stress after "to", "I", "will" or "don't", or before an object ("record it"). After "a", "the", "so" or "is" they take the noun/adjective stress. With no clue they take the noun/adjective stress, the more common one. A small table does the same for vowel changes: live, lead, wind, close, use.

          Then rhyme. If another reading makes a perfect or near rhyme within reach and the grammatical one doesn't, it wins. That's how rappers bend words, and the only way to tell present "read" from past "read". A clear grammar clue can only be overruled by a perfect rhyme or a line-ending rhyme. Small words like "a" and "the" never switch.

          3Syllables and rhyme partswhat part of a syllable carries the rhyme

          Each word's sounds are split into syllables, one vowel each. A syllable's rhyme part is its vowel plus every consonant up to the next vowel. A consonant between two vowels counts for both syllables, which is how "squeal-ers" can rhyme with "wheels".

          Stress matters too. The dictionary marks each vowel as stressed, lightly stressed or unstressed. Unstressed /ə/, /ɪ/ and /ʊ/ all sound like a schwa, so they count as one sound.

          Mapping sounds back onto letters is approximate. It's how the colours land on "spin" and "nin'" rather than on the whole word.

          4How close is close enoughperfect, near and assonance

          Two rhyme parts are compared using Pat Pattison's songwriting categories:

          • Perfect: same vowel and the same consonants after it (grease / peace).
          • Near: same vowel; consonants from the same family (stops, fricatives, nasals, ignoring voicing) or one extra consonant (time / line, wheels / squealers).
          • Assonance: the same vowel alone (sit / spin). Rap analysts treat this as the backbone of modern rhyme, so it counts, but it's drawn lighter.

          The vowels of "cot" and "caught" count as one, as in most rap accents, except before r: "door" and "car" don't rhyme.

          The Rhymes switch above the verse sets the loosest category allowed.

          5Who gets comparedstress decides what can rhyme on its own

          Only syllables a listener hears as beats are compared on their own: stressed syllables of content words, the last syllable of every line, and full unstressed endings like the -y of "sweaty".

          Function words ("the", "a", "on") and weak syllables can still be coloured, but only as part of a longer multisyllabic rhyme. Without this rule every "the" would rhyme with every "a".

          Two syllables can link only if they're within Reach lines of each other (four by default) and in the same section. A blank line or a heading like [Chorus] starts a new section.

          6Multisyllabic rhymesruns of syllables that match in order

          A multisyllabic rhyme is two runs of syllables whose rhyme parts match position by position, across word boundaries. Malmi et al. measure rhyme the same way, as the longest matching vowel sequence.

          A run starts on a stressed syllable on both sides. Stressed positions must match at the chosen category, and two unstressed schwas may stand in for each other. One weak syllable in the middle may break the pattern, because performers swallow those. A run needs at least two positions that carry the rhyme, and repeating the same words doesn't count.

          Each run is underlined in one colour so it reads as a unit. Back-to-back repeats of a pattern on one line merge into a single underline.

          7Groups and coloursevery link joins a group; groups get colours

          Links, single or inside a multi, join syllables into rhyme groups, closest match first. Groups chain: if A rhymes with B and B with C, all three share a colour. But a looser link never merges two groups that each rhyme more closely on their own, so "peace / lease" and "keep / deep" stay two colours though they share a vowel, and one vowel can't spread across a whole verse. A looser link can still bring a lone syllable into a group.

          A group is named by the rhyme part, in IPA, that most of its members share. If none covers more than half, it's named by the vowel alone. A stress mark shows when most of its syllables are stressed: /ˈɪn/ for "spin / grin", plain /ɪn/ for the unstressed "-nin' / -nin'".

          Colours come from a 14-colour palette built for both light and dark themes. Groups within reach of each other always get different colours. A letter in the margin marks each line's end rhyme, A, B and so on, with x for a line ending that doesn't rhyme. Letters start again at A in each section.

          ?Where the sources agree, and where they don'twhy some rules are switches

          Published ways of coding rap rhymes agree that the stressed vowel carries the rhyme. Raplyzer measures nothing else, and the Vox / Martin Connor breakdowns and Wadsworth & Needle treat assonance as rhyme. Hirjee & Brown and the WSJ's Hamilton analysis score vowels first. They also agree that multisyllabic rhymes are matching vowel sequences shown as one unit, and that stress decides which syllables anchor a rhyme.

          They disagree on consonants: Raplyzer ignores them, Hirjee & Brown and the WSJ score them by similarity, and Wadsworth & Needle's main class requires the final consonant. That's why there are three categories. They disagree on repetition: Wadsworth & Needle count an "identical rhyme", while Raplyzer's code scores a repeated word as zero. That's why "A word rhymes with itself" is a switch.
